\subsection{Where Sensor Over-Reliance Comes From}
A fusion model can lean on one sensor because its architecture or supervision favours that sensor, or because the training data make that sensor sufficient. DGFusion~\cite{dgfusion2026} is a useful test case: it adds depth supervision to its fusion, and removing depth costs our reproduction of DGFusion 23 to 37 points depending on the condition (Fig.~\ref{fig:dependence}). If the depth supervision caused this dependence, a model without it should behave differently.

Our model trained on clean inputs, which has no depth supervision, also depends on depth: removing it costs 14.3 to 14.8 points in sun, cloud, rain, and fog, 17.9 at night, and 25.0 at low exposure, less than for DGFusion but growing towards the dark conditions in the same way. Our full model, trained with the C2D2 scheme, loses only 0.6 to 1.5 points in sun, cloud, rain, and fog, 4.3 at night, and 9.6 at low exposure (all on the test split). The effect also appears in a second architecture: when we re-train DGFusion with the same synthetic degradation, removing depth entirely costs 4.7 points, whereas it costs 33.3 points for our reproduction trained without degradation and 33.4 for the released weights (test split, our evaluation code).

These measurements suggest that, on DELIVER, the dependence on depth comes mainly from training without degraded sensors rather than from a particular fusion design or supervision, since it appears in two architectures and falls in both when degraded inputs enter training (Fig.~\ref{fig:dependence} and the re-trained DGFusion above). A plausible reason is that DELIVER renders depth without noise or holes, so training on complete inputs finds a shortcut that real depth sensors would not offer. The comparison has one caveat: our two curves differ in training and also in the high-resolution path, which only the full model has; on the validation split, the C2D2-trained model without the path loses 3.4 to 13.4 points, so most of the reduction comes from the training.

Zero-filling a sensor at test time measures how much a trained model relies on it, not how much accuracy the sensor adds. To measure the latter, we train our model from the start without depth, on RGB, events, and LiDAR (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:train}). It reaches 55.5 test mIoU, 3.9 points above DGFusion with the same three sensors (51.6~\cite{dgfusion2026}), and adding depth raises our accuracy by about two points, against 5.1 points for DGFusion (from 51.6 to 56.7~\cite{dgfusion2026}). Our model therefore stays accurate when depth is never available and gains less from it.

\subsection{What the High-Resolution Path Adds}
The high-resolution path raises clean test mIoU by 1.15 points (from 56.59 to 57.74 on the test split), and by 1.03 points for the model trained without C2D2 (from 56.40 to 57.43), so its gain does not depend on the training scheme. Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:ladder} lists the IoU of every class without and with the path; the scene with the largest gain on each thin class is shown in Fig.~\ref{S-fig:thin_scenes} (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:qual}). Static gains 10.4 points, Pedestrian 3.8, TrafficSign 3.1, and Pole 2.1, and the four thin classes gain 3.0 points together. Against the released baselines, evaluated with our code, our model is ahead of all three on 16 of the 25 classes. The largest margins are on Wall (+11.3 points), TrafficSign (+8.0), Water (+6.8), Truck (+5.5), and Static (+5.4). On Wall, TrafficSign, Truck, Water, Building, and Dynamic, the model without the path is already ahead of every baseline, by 9.2, 4.9, 4.4, 2.8, 1.8, and 1.2 points; Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:ladder} splits these margins over the stages of the model. On Static, SideWalk, Cars, and GroundRail, the model without the path is behind the best baseline, and the path turns this into a lead; on Static it adds 10.4 points. This matches what the path changes in the architecture: it computes features at stride 4 from the raw pixels, whereas every other input of the decoder is interpolated from stride-16 tokens (Section~\ref{sec:m_decoder}). Measured on the test set, the magnitude of its stride-4 features is 2.5 times higher on pixels of the four thin classes than on average over the image, against 0.93 for the tapped backbone features at the same level, and switching the path off at inference, a configuration not seen in training, costs the thin classes 2.2 times as much IoU as the other classes (1.4 times when the path is switched off for the dense head only, so part of the first ratio reflects the unseen configuration). Fig.~\ref{fig:hires} shows why: a pole, a traffic light, or a distant pedestrian is narrower than the 16-pixel cell of one token, so the backbone features at stride 16 show only blocks, whereas the path responds along the poles, power lines, traffic-light housings, and sign edges that the prediction with the path recovers. The concentration ratio stays between 2.46 and 2.58 across the five conditions. The baselines stay ahead on RailTrack, where DGFusion reaches 64.5 and our model varies strongly between training runs, and on the thin vertical classes TrafficLight, Pole, and Pedestrian, where CAFuser is best; the high-resolution path narrows the gap on Pole and Pedestrian but does not close it, and it widens the gap on TrafficLight (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:failure}). Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:ladder} traces both losses to the stage where they appear. The gain comes mainly from recall: for Pole the recall rises from 0.75 to 0.78 and for Pedestrian from 0.89 to 0.90 (pixels pooled over the test set). Two classes lose: TrafficLight by 4.1 points and RailTrack by 7.2. RailTrack varies strongly between training runs even without the path, so we do not interpret its change. The TrafficLight loss is analysed in Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:failure}.

\subsection{Limitations}
First, DELIVER is synthetic, and its complete depth is the reason the shortcut exists; the size of the effect on real depth sensors remains to be measured, and MUSES does not contain depth. Second, inference takes 2.9 times the latency of DGFusion (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Sharing tokens across sensors would reduce this cost.
