\subsection{Where Sensor Over-Reliance Comes From}
A fusion model can lean on one sensor because its architecture or supervision favours that sensor, or because the training data make that sensor sufficient. DGFusion~\cite{dgfusion2026} is a useful test case: it adds depth supervision to its fusion, and removing depth costs our reproduction of DGFusion 23 to 37 points depending on the condition (Fig.~\ref{fig:dependence}). If the depth supervision caused this dependence, a model without it should behave differently.

Our model trained on clean inputs, which has no depth supervision, also depends on depth: removing it costs 14.3 to 14.8 points in sun, cloud, rain, and fog, 17.9 at night, and 25.0 at low exposure, less than for DGFusion but growing towards the dark conditions in the same way. Our full model, trained with the C2D2 scheme, loses only 0.6 to 1.5 points in sun, cloud, rain, and fog, 4.3 at night, and 9.6 at low exposure (all on the test split; three seeds for the full model, two for the clean-trained one). The effect also appears in a second architecture: when we re-train DGFusion with the same synthetic degradation, removing depth entirely costs 4.7 points, whereas it costs 33.3 points for our reproduction trained without degradation and 33.4 for the released weights (test split, our evaluation code).

% judgment-ledger:117 (reproduction (a) -> retrained (b): depth fully missing -33.3 -> -4.7); judgment-ledger:130 (official weights -33.4).

These measurements suggest that, on DELIVER, the dependence on depth comes mainly from training without degraded sensors rather than from a particular fusion design or supervision, since it appears in two architectures and falls in both when degraded inputs enter training (Fig.~\ref{fig:dependence} and the re-trained DGFusion above). A plausible reason is that DELIVER renders depth without noise or holes, so training on complete inputs finds a shortcut that real depth sensors would not offer. The comparison has one caveat: our two curves differ in training and also in the high-resolution path, which only the full model has; an earlier measurement on the validation split of the C2D2-trained model without the path (3.4 to 13.4 points) indicates that most of the reduction comes from the training.

\subsection{What the High-Resolution Path Adds}
The high-resolution path raises clean test mIoU by 1.15 points on average over three seeds (from 56.59 to 57.74 on the test split). Fig.~\ref{fig:thin} shows where the gain comes from. Averaged over the three seeds, Static gains 10.4 points, Pedestrian 3.8, TrafficSign 3.1, and Pole 2.1, and the four thin classes gain 3.0 points together (Table~\ref{tab:thin}). Table~\ref{tab:thin} compares every class with the released baselines. Our model is ahead of all three on 16 of the 25 classes, and on 15 of them every one of its three seeds is ahead. The largest margins are on Wall (+11.3 points), TrafficSign (+8.0), Water (+6.8), Truck (+5.5), and Static (+5.4). On Wall, TrafficSign, Truck, Water, Building, and Dynamic, the model without the path is already ahead of every baseline in all three seeds, by 9.2, 4.9, 4.4, 2.8, 1.8, and 1.2 points; Appendix~\ref{app:ladder} splits these margins over the stages of the model. On Static, SideWalk, Cars, and GroundRail, the model without the path is behind the best baseline in every seed, and the path turns this into a lead; on Static it adds 10.4 points. This matches what the path changes in the architecture: it computes features at stride 4 from the raw pixels, whereas every other input of the decoder is interpolated from stride-16 tokens (Section~\ref{sec:m_decoder}). Measured on the test set, the magnitude of its stride-4 features is 2.5 times higher on pixels of the four thin classes than on average over the image, against 0.93 for the tapped backbone features at the same level, and switching the path off at inference, a configuration not seen in training, costs the thin classes 2.2 times as much IoU as the other classes (1.4 times when the path is switched off for the dense head only, so part of the first ratio reflects the unseen configuration). Fig.~\ref{fig:hires} shows the two feature maps: the tapped features follow the 16-pixel token grid, while the path responds along lane markings, poles, bicycles, and window frames. The concentration ratio stays between 2.46 and 2.58 across the five conditions. The baselines stay ahead on RailTrack, where DGFusion reaches 64.5 and our seeds vary between 26 and 57, and on the thin vertical classes TrafficLight, Pole, and Pedestrian, where CAFuser is best; the high-resolution path narrows the gap on Pole and Pedestrian but does not close it, and it widens the gap on TrafficLight (Section~\ref{sec:failure_tl}). Appendix~\ref{app:ladder} traces both losses to the stage where they appear. The gain comes mainly from recall: for Pole the recall rises from 0.75 to 0.78 and for Pedestrian from 0.89 to 0.90 (three seeds, pixels pooled over the test set). Two classes lose: TrafficLight by 4.1 points and RailTrack by 7.2. RailTrack varies strongly between seeds even without the path (58.5, 32.6, and 45.1 for the three seeds), so we do not interpret its change. The TrafficLight loss, in contrast, is systematic and is analysed next.

\subsection{Failure Case: Posters Read as Traffic Lights}\label{sec:failure_tl}
With the high-resolution path, the recall of TrafficLight rises (0.80 to 0.85) but its precision falls (0.47 to 0.40). Fig.~\ref{fig:failure} shows where the extra false positives appear. In the scenes with the largest increase, billboards and posters, labelled as \emph{Other}, are painted as traffic lights, and in one rainy scene a traffic sign is. The two classes share fine structure, a small bright rectangular shape on a pole or a wall, but differ in context. Because the high-resolution features are added to every pyramid level without a gate, fine detail reaches the dense per-pixel classifier directly, and the classifier can follow the detail without enough context. This mechanism is a hypothesis that our measurements are consistent with but do not prove; we discuss a remedy in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Scenes Where a Baseline Is Better}
Fig.~\ref{fig:reverse} in Appendix~\ref{app:qual} applies the selection rule of Fig.~\ref{fig:adverse} in the opposite direction: for each condition it shows the test scene where another model leads ours by the largest per-image mIoU. In four of the six conditions that model is DGFusion and in two it is CAFuser, and the gap is 8.5 to 12.9 points, similar in size to the largest gaps in our favour (3.9 to 13.0 points in Fig.~\ref{fig:adverse}). These scenes are not chosen to be typical; they bound how much a single image can favour a baseline when all sensors are present. The losses fall into two groups. In sun and cloud, small signs are labelled as traffic lights and parts of buildings as poles, the failure of the high-resolution path discussed in Section~\ref{sec:failure_tl}. Under low exposure, night, and fog, large regions take a wrong class: a building is read as sky at low exposure, a fence as a wall at night, and a static object as a truck in fog. In rain the loss is spread along the boundary between vegetation and terrain.


\subsection{Limitations}
First, DELIVER is synthetic, and its complete depth is the reason the shortcut exists; the size of the effect on real depth sensors remains to be measured, and MUSES does not contain depth. Second, our main runs use a 40-epoch schedule and three seeds, the leave-one-out ablation two seeds, and the baselines are single runs, so differences below about one point should not be over-read. Third, whether the high-resolution path, whose convolutional stem sees raw pixels, changes robustness to pixel noise has not been measured: under the benchmark noise our model keeps 58.63, 52.00, and 39.87 mIoU at the three levels (Table~\ref{tab:robust}), but the same measurement without the path is missing. Fourth, the frozen ViT-L processes every sensor separately, so inference costs 14.6 times the operations and 2.9 times the latency of DGFusion (Table~\ref{tab:cost}); a smaller backbone reduces this but not for free: with DINOv3-B the operations fall to 40\% and the latency by half, while test mIoU falls to 53.39, below DGFusion. A large part of the accuracy advantage therefore comes from the large frozen backbone, whereas the benchmark models of Table~\ref{tab:robust} use MiT-B2. Sharing tokens across sensors would also reduce the cost, which we have not evaluated.
