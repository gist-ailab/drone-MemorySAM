\subsection{Where Sensor Over-Reliance Comes From}
A fusion model can lean on one sensor because its architecture or supervision favours that sensor, or because the training data make that sensor sufficient. DGFusion~\cite{dgfusion2026} is a useful test case: it adds depth supervision to its fusion, and removing depth costs the released DGFusion model 26 to 41 points depending on the condition (Fig.~\ref{fig:dependence}). If the depth supervision caused this dependence, a model without it should behave differently.

Our model trained on clean inputs, which has no depth supervision, also depends on depth: removing it costs 14.3 to 14.8 points in sun, cloud, rain, and fog, 17.9 at night, and 25.0 at low exposure, less than for DGFusion but growing towards the dark conditions in the same way. Our full model, trained with the C2D2 scheme, loses only 0.6 to 1.5 points in sun, cloud, rain, and fog, 4.3 at night, and 9.6 at low exposure (all on the test split). The effect also appears in a second architecture: when we re-train DGFusion with the same synthetic degradation, removing depth entirely costs 4.7 points, whereas it costs 33.3 points for our reproduction trained without degradation and 33.4 for the released weights (test split, our evaluation code).

These measurements suggest that, on DELIVER, the dependence on depth comes mainly from training without degraded sensors rather than from a particular fusion design or supervision, since it appears in two architectures and falls in both when degraded inputs enter training (Fig.~\ref{fig:dependence} and the re-trained DGFusion above). A plausible reason is that DELIVER renders depth without noise or holes, so training on complete inputs finds a shortcut that real depth sensors would not offer. The comparison has one caveat: our two curves differ in training and also in the high-resolution path, which only the full model has; on the validation split, the C2D2-trained model without the path loses 3.4 to 13.4 points, so most of the reduction comes from the training.

Zero-filling a sensor at test time measures how much a trained model relies on it, not how much accuracy the sensor adds. To measure the latter, we train our model from the start without depth, on RGB, events, and LiDAR (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:train}). It reaches 55.5 test mIoU, 3.9 points above DGFusion with the same three sensors (51.6~\cite{dgfusion2026}), and adding depth raises our accuracy by about two points, against 5.1 points for DGFusion (from 51.6 to 56.7~\cite{dgfusion2026}). Our model therefore stays accurate when depth is never available and gains less from it.

\subsection{Effect of the High-Resolution Path}
The high-resolution path (Section~\ref{sec:m_decoder}) raises clean test mIoU by 1.15 points (from 56.59 to 57.74), and by 1.03 points for the model trained without C2D2 (from 56.40 to 57.43), so its gain does not depend on the training scheme. The gain is concentrated on thin and small objects: Static gains 10.4 points, Pedestrian 3.8, TrafficSign 3.1, and Pole 2.1, and the four thin classes of Fig.~\ref{fig:hires} gain 3.0 points together. It comes mainly from recall, which rises from 0.75 to 0.78 for Pole and from 0.89 to 0.90 for Pedestrian (pixels pooled over the test set). This matches what the path changes in the architecture: measured on the test set, the magnitude of its stride-4 features is 2.5 times higher on pixels of the four thin classes than on the other pixels, against 0.93 for the tapped backbone features at the same level, and the ratio stays between 2.46 and 2.58 across the five conditions. Against the released baselines, evaluated with our code, our model is ahead of all three on 16 of the 25 classes, with the largest margins on Wall (+11.3 points), TrafficSign (+8.0), Water (+6.8), Truck (+5.5), and Static (+5.4); on Static, SideWalk, Cars, and GroundRail, the path turns a deficit into a lead. Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:ladder} lists every class for the baselines and for each stage of our model, Fig.~\ref{S-fig:thin_scenes} shows the scene with the largest gain on each thin class, and Supplementary Section~\ref{S-app:failure} analyses the classes on which the path loses accuracy.

\subsection{Limitations}
First, DELIVER is synthetic, and its complete depth is a plausible reason for the shortcut; the size of the effect on real depth sensors remains to be measured, and MUSES does not contain depth. Second, inference takes 2.9 times the latency of DGFusion (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Sharing tokens across sensors would reduce this cost.
